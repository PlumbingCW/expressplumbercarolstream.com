Epoxy Pipe Lining: Pros and Cons for Carol Stream, IL Homes

Pipe lining promises a new pipe inside the old one, with little digging. It can be a smart fix or a poor fit, depending on the pipe. This guide sets out the advantages, the limits and the questions to ask.

What pipe lining is

A liner is a flexible tube saturated with resin. It is pulled or inflated into the old pipe and cured so it hardens into a new pipe inside the old one. The result is a smooth, jointless inner surface that seals cracks and stops root entry.

The method is used on sewer laterals and, in some cases, on drain lines inside buildings. It is a trenchless method, which means little digging.

The advantages

  • Little excavation, so lawns and driveways stay intact.
  • A smooth interior that improves flow.
  • Seals joints where roots enter.
  • Often faster than open-trench replacement.
  • Can pass through bends in many cases.

These benefits matter most when the pipe runs under mature landscaping or paving that would be costly to restore.

The limits

  • It cannot fix a collapsed or badly offset pipe.
  • The pipe must be cleaned thoroughly before lining.
  • It reduces the inner diameter slightly.
  • Active laterals must be reinstated by cutting openings.
  • Quality depends on installer skill and the resin.

A camera inspection first is essential. Without it, no one knows if lining will work.

Questions to ask a contractor

  1. What does the camera inspection show, and can I see the video.
  2. What resin and liner thickness will you use.
  3. How long is the warranty and what does it cover.
  4. How will you reinstate side connections.
  5. Is a permit needed and who pulls it.

Ask for the answers in writing. Compare quotes on the same scope.

Lining or repiping inside the house

Lining is also used for supply lines in some buildings, but supply pipe lining is less common and has its own concerns. Replacement is often simpler for a house with failing copper or galvanized pipe, as described under repiping.

For a damaged sewer lateral in towns such as West Chicago or Wheaton, lining can be a good choice if the camera supports it.

The preparation that makes or breaks a liner

The old pipe must be cleaned down to the pipe wall before lining. Roots are cut out, scale is removed and any standing water is cleared. A liner that goes over debris will not bond or will wrinkle.

Ask how the contractor cleans and how they verify the result. A second camera pass before lining is a good sign.

Lifespan and warranty

  • Manufacturers often claim fifty years or more under ideal conditions.
  • Real life depends on installation quality.
  • Warranties vary in length and in what they cover.
  • Ask whether the warranty transfers when you sell.
  • Get the terms in writing.

A warranty is only as good as the company that gives it. Check that the contractor has been in business a while.

Effect on resale and inspections

A lined lateral can be a plus when a buyer's inspector scopes the line and sees a clean, continuous pipe. Keep the video and the warranty with your home records.

Some buyers still prefer a replaced pipe. Neither choice is wrong. Match the method to the pipe's condition.

What the process looks like on site

  1. The contractor runs a camera to map the pipe and mark the problem spots.
  2. The pipe is cleaned with cutters or a jet.
  3. The liner is saturated with resin and loaded into the pipe.
  4. The liner is inflated and cured with heat, steam or light.
  5. Side connections are reopened and a final camera run checks the result.

Plan for the toilets and sinks to be off for part of a day. Ask the contractor how long the curing step takes.

Signs that lining is the wrong choice

  • The pipe has collapsed or is badly offset.
  • A section has a large belly that holds water.
  • The pipe is crushed or sheared by settling.
  • The diameter is too small for a liner to pass.
  • The camera cannot pass because of a blockage.

In those cases, replacement is the sound choice. A good contractor tells you so even if it means a smaller job.

A short decision guide

  1. Get a camera inspection and a copy of the video.
  2. Ask the plumber whether the pipe is a candidate for lining.
  3. Request quotes for lining and for replacement.
  4. Compare warranties and what each covers.
  5. Choose the method that fits the pipe and budget.

Rushing into either choice is a mistake. Make the camera drive the decision.

Who should consider lining

Lining suits owners whose lateral runs under a driveway, patio or mature landscaping. It also suits older clay or cast iron pipes that are cracked but still aligned and round.

If your yard is open and the run is short, simple excavation and replacement may cost less. Get quotes for both approaches before deciding.

LOCAL NOTE · CAROL STREAM, IL

Permits, utilities and calling before you dig

Carol Stream's Public Works, at 630-871-6260, can tell you where the village main and your service connections lie. Ask the Village of Carol Stream building staff whether a permit applies to lining or replacement.

Illinois requires you to call JULIE before excavation so underground utilities can be marked. Lining reduces digging, but pits for access still need marking.

Questions readers ask

Do you inspect sewers in Carol Stream before recommending lining?

Yes. A camera inspection comes first.

Does lining last as long as a new pipe?

Manufacturers often claim long life. Ask about the warranty.

Can a collapsed pipe be lined?

Usually not. Pipe bursting or replacement is needed.

Does lining reduce flow?

Slightly, but the smooth surface often offsets it.

How long does lining take?

Many jobs take a day or two once the pipe is cleaned.
