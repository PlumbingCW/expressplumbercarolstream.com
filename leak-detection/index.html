Start with the symptom, not the pipe

Most people do not call because they suspect a leak. They call because the water meter keeps turning with every tap off, the bill jumped, a warm patch appeared on the floor, paint is bubbling on a ceiling, or the water pressure fell without explanation. Leak detection begins with those clues and works backward to the pipe.

A plumber asks when the symptom started, which rooms it affects and whether anything changed nearby. A stain under a second-floor bathroom points to a drain, a supply line or a shower pan. A wet crawl space wall points to a different set of causes than a wet ceiling in a kitchen.

The meter test you can do yourself

You can learn a lot in ten minutes. Turn off every fixture, the ice maker and the irrigation system. Find the water meter and read the dial or the low-flow indicator. If it moves with everything off, water is escaping somewhere between the meter and the fixtures.

  1. Turn off every tap, appliance and sprinkler.
  2. Read the meter and note the number.
  3. Wait 30 minutes without using any water.
  4. Read it again and compare.
  5. Close the main valve inside the house and recheck: if the meter still moves, the leak is on the service line before the valve.

That last step separates a leak in the house from a leak in the buried line, and it saves the plumber time. See water line repair if the loss is outside the house.

How leaks are found without tearing the wall open

The tools depend on the symptom. Acoustic listening devices pick up the hiss of pressurized water in a pipe. Thermal imaging shows temperature differences on a wall or floor. Moisture meters map how far dampness has spread. Pressure testing isolates which branch of the plumbing is losing pressure.

Ceiling, wall and under-house leaks

A bathroom on a second floor, a laundry room over a finished basement and a kitchen above a crawl space all hide pipes. Water follows the path of least resistance, so the stain is rarely under the leak. The plumber traces upstream from the damage and tests each possible source.

Basement and crawl space moisture is also the place where plumbing leaks get confused with groundwater. A damp wall after a heavy rain is a drainage problem. A damp wall in a dry week is more likely a pipe. The basement waterproofing page explains how to tell the difference and what to do about each.

A real local reason to check early

The Village of Carol Stream states in its 2025 water quality report that the village does not have lead service lines in its system, and that a service line material inventory is available from Public Works at 630-871-6260. That is good news about the street side of the meter. It does not describe pipes inside your home, which depend on the age and materials of the house.

Pipes in homes from the village's earliest decades may be copper or galvanized steel, and newer homes may have plastic supply. Leak detection reveals which material you actually have behind the drywall, and that helps you plan whether a spot repair or a repiping project makes sense.

Leak Detection questions

Do you handle leak detection emergencies at night or on weekends in Carol Stream?

Yes. The 24/7 line takes active leak calls any time. Shut off the main water valve if water is spreading and call, and the dispatcher sends a licensed plumber the same day when available.

How can I tell if I have a hidden leak?

Watch for a water meter that moves with everything off, an unexplained jump in the bill, warm spots in the floor, musty smells, or stains and bubbling paint.

Will finding the leak damage my walls?

Usually little. Listening and thermal tools narrow the location so any opening is small and targeted.

Is a leak in the ground outside covered by the same service?

Yes. The plumber can test the service line between the meter and the house and explain the repair options.

Does homeowners insurance cover a hidden leak?

Policies vary. Many cover sudden water damage but not slow leaks or the pipe itself, so read your policy or call your insurer before you start repairs.
